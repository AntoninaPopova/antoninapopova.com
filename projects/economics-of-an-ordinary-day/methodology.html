01 / RESEARCH OBJECTIVE

Research objective

The Economics of an Ordinary Day examines the distribution of daily time between women and men, with particular attention to unpaid work and its economic implications.

The project explores how differences in daily time allocation accumulate over longer periods, how unpaid work can be expressed in monetary terms, and what alternative economic uses of this time might represent.

The analysis is organised around four complementary perspectives:

  1. Time allocation. The composition of an average 24-hour day and differences in unpaid-work time between women and men.
  2. Accumulation. The cumulative magnitude of daily differences over a selected period.
  3. Valuation. The estimated monetary value of unpaid work using alternative occupational wage benchmarks.
  4. Opportunity cost. Hypothetical additional earnings associated with reallocating a share of unpaid-work time to paid employment.

The project is descriptive and exploratory. It does not seek to establish causal relationships or predict individual labour-market outcomes.

03 / DATA PREPARATION

From source to dataset

The original datasets were processed and combined using Python and the pandas library.

Selection of time-use observations

The latest available time-use observation for each country was retained. Consequently, the analysis does not represent a single common survey year across all countries.

The original observations were expressed in minutes per day and converted into hours by dividing each value by 60.

Hours per day = Minutes per day / 60

The five activity categories already summed to 24 hours in the retained observations. No proportional adjustment or additional normalisation was required.

Selection of earnings observations

Hourly earnings were selected for women and men in each of the three occupational categories.

Earnings were retained in 2021 international dollars, adjusted for purchasing power parity, as provided directly by ILOSTAT. No additional currency conversion or purchasing-power adjustment was applied.

Country coverage and missing observations

The two datasets were combined using country and gender as matching dimensions.

The final analytical sample was restricted to countries with the required observations in both datasets. Countries with missing time-use or required earnings data were excluded.

No missing observations were imputed. The geographical coverage of the project therefore reflects data availability rather than a predefined representative sample of countries.

Calculation of a common hourly wage

For each country and occupational category, a common hourly wage was calculated as the simple arithmetic mean of female and male hourly earnings.

Wc = Ww + Wm 2

Here, Ww and Wm represent female and male hourly earnings, respectively, and Wc represents the common hourly wage.

This is an unweighted arithmetic mean, not an employment-weighted estimate of average earnings across the workforce.

Applying the same wage to both groups allows the monetary effect of differences in unpaid-work hours to be examined independently of differences in the selected hourly earnings benchmarks.

04 / ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK

Analytical framework

The four chapters use a common set of time-use observations and progressively introduce additional analytical parameters.

Notation

Symbol Definition
g Population group: women or men
Hg Average daily unpaid-work hours for group g
Wg Selected hourly earnings benchmark for group g
Y Projection period in years
s Share of unpaid-work time hypothetically reallocated to paid employment
w, m Women and men, respectively

CHAPTER 01 / THE DAY

Daily time allocation

The first chapter presents the composition of an average 24-hour day for women and men in the selected country.

The stacked bars represent the duration of each activity category. They do not represent a chronological schedule or indicate when particular activities occur during the day.

The daily unpaid-work gap is calculated as the difference between female and male average daily unpaid-work hours.

ΔH = Hw − Hm

A positive difference indicates that women spend more time on unpaid work; a negative difference indicates that men spend more time on unpaid work.

The visualisation expresses the magnitude of the difference in hours and minutes, with its direction communicated through the accompanying text.

CHAPTER 02 / ACCUMULATION

Accumulation over time

The second chapter examines how daily unpaid-work hours accumulate over a selected period of one to ten years.

Accumulated unpaid-work hours for each group are calculated as:

Ag = Hg × 365 × Y

The cumulative difference between women and men is therefore:

ΔA = (Hw − Hm) × 365 × Y

The calculation assumes 365 days per year and constant average daily unpaid-work hours throughout the selected projection period.

Leap years, demographic changes and changes in household responsibilities or individual time-use patterns are not modelled.

Illustrative time comparisons

To communicate the magnitude of accumulated hours, the visualisation relates selected results to familiar activities, such as reading, personal projects, academic studies and professional experience.

These comparisons are based on illustrative workload assumptions. Educational comparisons are informed by typical academic workload estimates.

They are intended as interpretive devices rather than precise measures of educational attainment, professional experience or achievable individual outcomes.

CHAPTER 03 / VALUATION

Monetary valuation of unpaid work

The third chapter applies an hourly earnings benchmark to accumulated unpaid-work hours in order to estimate their monetary value.

Vg = Hg × 365 × Y × Wg

The reader can select one of three occupational wage benchmarks and choose between two wage assumptions.

Wage assumption Calculation
Gender-specific wages Female and male hourly earnings are applied separately to the corresponding unpaid-work hours.
Common hourly wage The arithmetic mean of female and male hourly earnings is applied to both groups.

The difference in estimated monetary value is calculated as:

ΔV = Vw − Vm

Under the common-wage assumption, the monetary difference reflects differences in unpaid-work hours alone.

Under the gender-specific wage assumption, the monetary difference reflects both differences in unpaid-work hours and differences in the selected hourly earnings benchmarks.

The resulting values are imputed monetary estimates. They do not represent actual payments, observed earnings or measured contributions to gross domestic product.

CHAPTER 04 / OPPORTUNITY

Alternative uses of time

The fourth chapter explores the potential additional earnings associated with a hypothetical reallocation of unpaid-work time to paid employment.

The reader selects a reallocation percentage between 0% and 100%, applied equally to the unpaid-work hours of women and men.

Reallocated hours are calculated as:

Rg = Hg × 365 × Y × s

Potential additional earnings are calculated by applying the selected hourly earnings benchmark to the reallocated hours.

Eg = Hg × 365 × Y × s × Wg

The difference in potential additional earnings is:

ΔE = Ew − Em

The country, projection period, occupational benchmark and wage assumption are inherited from the preceding chapters.

The default reallocation share of 25% is an illustrative starting point, not an empirically estimated proportion of unpaid work that could realistically be reallocated.

A reallocation of 100% represents a theoretical upper-bound scenario within the selected model assumptions. It does not imply that all unpaid work can or should be eliminated.

The scenario assumes that reallocated hours can be converted into paid employment at the selected hourly earnings benchmark. Employment availability, taxation, replacement care costs and other constraints are not modelled.

05 / MONETARY INTERPRETATION

Interpreting monetary estimates

Purchasing power parity

All monetary values are expressed in 2021 international dollars, adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP).

Purchasing power parity accounts for differences in price levels across countries and provides a more comparable monetary unit than conversion using market exchange rates alone.

The dollar symbol used in the visualisation therefore refers to international dollars in PPP terms, not nominal US dollars converted at market exchange rates.

The year 2021 identifies the purchasing power reference of the monetary unit. It should not be interpreted as the reference year of every time-use observation.

Choice of wage benchmark

There is no single universally applicable market price for unpaid work.

Household production and care encompass a wide range of activities that differ in their nature, skill requirements and possible market substitutes.

Rather than assign a definitive monetary value to unpaid work, the project allows readers to explore how estimates change under alternative occupational wage benchmarks.

The selected benchmarks are general earnings proxies. They do not constitute a task-specific replacement-cost valuation of household production.

Valuation and opportunity cost

Chapters 3 and 4 apply the same underlying hourly earnings benchmarks but address different economic questions.

Chapter 3 asks what unpaid-work hours might represent in monetary terms under a selected valuation assumption.

Chapter 4 asks what a share of those hours might represent in potential additional earnings if it became available for paid employment.

Because both calculations use the same earnings benchmark, their results are mathematically related:

Eg = s × Vg

At a hypothetical reallocation of 100%, the numerical estimates in both chapters are equal under identical assumptions. Their economic interpretations, however, remain distinct.

In economic theory, opportunity cost refers to the value of the next-best alternative forgone. Paid employment represents only one possible alternative use of time, alongside education, rest, leisure and other activities.

The opportunity-cost exploration therefore illustrates a specific potential earnings scenario rather than measuring the full economic opportunity cost of unpaid work.

06 / LIMITATIONS

Assumptions and limitations

International comparability

Time-use statistics originate from national surveys that may differ in their reference periods, survey design, population coverage and classification of activities.

Although the OECD harmonises observations into broad activity categories, differences in national statistical methodologies may affect international comparability.

Temporal comparability

The analysis uses the latest available time-use observation for each country. These observations may refer to different survey years.

Time-use and earnings observations may also refer to different periods. The combined dataset should therefore not be interpreted as a fully synchronised cross-country snapshot.

Population averages

The analysis is based on aggregate observations disaggregated by gender.

Average time-use patterns conceal substantial variation across individuals and households, including differences associated with age, employment status, income, household composition and care responsibilities.

The results describe group-level averages and should not be interpreted as the experience of every individual woman or man.

Differences in population coverage

The time-use and earnings datasets describe different statistical populations. Hourly earnings refer to employees, whereas time-use averages may also include individuals outside paid employment.

Applying employee earnings benchmarks to population-level unpaid-work hours is therefore a modelling assumption, not a direct measurement of the earnings available to each individual.

Projection assumptions

All multi-year calculations assume constant daily unpaid-work hours and constant hourly earnings benchmarks.

Wage growth, inflation dynamics, changes in productivity, demographic changes and behavioural responses are not explicitly modelled.

Monetary values are accumulated without discounting. They represent undiscounted scenario totals rather than present-value estimates.

Labour-market constraints

Additional time available for paid employment does not necessarily translate into additional earnings.

Employment opportunities, working-time arrangements, qualifications, geographic mobility and individual circumstances influence the extent to which additional hours can be converted into paid work.

The potential earnings scenarios do not incorporate these constraints or the possible costs of replacing unpaid household and care activities.

Scope of monetary valuation

Monetary estimates do not capture the full social, personal or economic significance of unpaid work.

Care, household production and other unpaid activities generate benefits that cannot necessarily be represented by a market wage.

The estimates should therefore be interpreted as one analytical perspective on unpaid work, rather than a comprehensive measure of its value.

Statistical uncertainty

The visualisation uses published point estimates. Sampling uncertainty, confidence intervals and statistical significance are not modelled.

Numerical differences should not be interpreted as evidence of statistically significant differences between countries or population groups.
